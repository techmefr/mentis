# internal-comms-async-pitfalls §5 — Agreements, documents and leaders

Norms that nobody wrote down are guessed, and the guesses differ. Documents and channels that nobody
maintains do worse than not exist.

## 5.1 Write the team's agreements down
1. **Author the agreements together**: working and communication styles, anticipated response times and
   preferred channels, kept in a place the team owns (GitHub's leaders' tips, which store them as files in
   the team's repository). Revisit them periodically, and always when a team forms or is reorganised.
2. **Be explicit about each person's expectations**: what you expect from them, what they can expect from
   each other and from you (GitHub).
3. **Say which standing meetings must be attended live and which can be caught up on asynchronously**, so
   people can take control of their time; meeting loads grow until it is unclear what is required and what
   is only for information (GitHub).
4. **Protect low-meeting time**: GitHub names no- and low-meeting days as an example and says to
   reschedule non-urgent meetings that fall on them.
5. **Keep sensitive notes apart.** GitHub's leaders mark sensitive parts of their weekly report private and
   share a public version more widely.

## 5.2 Documents and channels have owners and a cost
1. **Update documentation and its ownership information when teams are reorganised or code or process
   changes** (GitHub).
2. **Put the time in the plan.** GitHub says managers build documentation upkeep into work plans; the time
   is saved the next time someone reads it, and outdated or wrong documentation lowers confidence in internal tools and wastes time.
3. **Delete or simplify a document when its upkeep outweighs its benefit** (GitHub).
4. **Leave it better than you found it**: when you find an outdated page, fix it or file an item for the
   owning team (GitHub).
5. **Close out what is no longer used**: for a repository, GitHub's guide closes the open items, updates the
   description and archives it when a project is unfunded or a team is restructured; the same applies to a
   channel (§1.2).
6. **Give a team's space a current front page**: charter, communication channels and where the team sits in
   the organisation (GitHub, for a team repository).

## 5.3 What a leader does
1. **Let the report own the one-to-one agenda**: GitHub's leaders' tips say to be present in one-to-ones
   and let the team member drive the agenda. The Clef handbook (CC0) describes a weekly meeting of at least
   thirty minutes with an employee-set agenda that is neither a status check nor a performance
   evaluation, regular rather than rigid, with notes to follow up on.
2. **Ask direct questions** (GitHub's examples: "What support would be helpful?" and "What's the biggest
   risk I'm not seeing right now?"), and seek feedback proactively, for instance by asking a few people to
   review an announcement before it goes out.
3. **Be open about what you do not know**: say "I don't know" or "I can't answer that right now" (GitHub).
4. **Make your own work visible** where decisions need input or others want regular updates, without
   tracking every administrative task (GitHub).
5. **Say what can be skipped** (§5.1), and write announcements before meetings (§4.1).
6. **Psychological safety comes first, in GitHub's framing**: the guide says openness to feedback, admitting
   mistakes and a safe place to raise concerns let people work independently without waiting for approval.
   This is the source's claim of cause, not measured here.
