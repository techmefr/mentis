# oss-compliance-programme §1 — People and policy

> Section 1 of `skills/oss-compliance-programme`. Read it when a programme is being set up or its ownership is
> unclear. Source: `Example-Generic-Company-Policy-Text.md` of the OpenChain Reference-Material repository
> (commit `e79a0b7`, ISO/IEC 5230 template folder, English), re-read in full 2026-10-16. The source says of
> itself that it is an example and is not a complete, prescriptive or recommended policy. Sentences marked
> "own guidance" are ours.

1. **Write the policy down, in one place everyone can reach.** The source's policy states why the company uses
   open source and what the risks are, who the policy applies to, and where it is kept; new joiners are told of
   it at induction and the fact is recorded. Keep it short: what the company does with open source, who is
   responsible, and what happens when it is not followed.
2. **Name three roles, each with a person.** The source separates the person with overall responsibility for the
   programme (a board-level or equivalent owner), the lead who runs it day to day, and a contact for questions
   from outside the company. Each has a named person, not a team alias, and a way to be reached. Small
   companies may give two roles to one person; say so in the policy (own guidance).
3. **Give the roles time and money.** The source says each role is staffed, funded and given time to do the
   work, and that a person who thinks theirs is not must raise it with the lead and, if unresolved, with the
   owner. Keep an escalation path of this kind written, so a lack of resources is a recorded event.
4. **State the scope.** Which products and which ways of delivering software the programme covers. The source
   lets the lead consider, at the start of any new project, whether it should be in scope, and routes a
   proposal to change the scope through the owner; follow the same pattern: someone proposes, someone
   decides, the policy is updated.
5. **Decide what counts as "distributed" for you.** The source defines the software it covers as software
   given to third parties, with an optional extension for software made available through an interface. Whether
   a hosted product is inside depends on how your licences treat it, and that is for counsel, not for the
   policy author (own guidance).
6. **Keep a list of the licences you meet most.** For each, record how it treats distribution in binary form
   and in source form, whether the copyleft is strong or weak, what hosted use does, what happens when you modify
   the code, and whether attribution is required (the six columns of the source's list). Do not fill the list
   from memory: counsel or the licence text supplies each row, and the date it was checked goes in the row
   (own guidance, see `business/licence-compliance`).
7. **A licence not on the list goes to review.** No component under an unlisted licence is used until the lead
   has had it reviewed, with counsel when in doubt, and the outcome is written in the decision log (part 2).
   The source has the lead make a first assessment against written criteria and refer any doubt to counsel.
8. **Write acceptance criteria for code.** The source requires that only code meeting its quality, licensing,
   provenance and functional requirements is used, and that all code is approved before it is added. Write the
   criteria your company applies; they are yours to decide.
9. **Say who the outside advisers are and review them.** The source names its advisers (we do not carry the
   names) and has the lead review their usefulness at a fixed interval; the interval is the owner's decision.
