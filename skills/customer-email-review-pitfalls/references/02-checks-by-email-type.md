# customer-email-review-pitfalls §2 — Checks by email type

> Part 2 of `skills/customer-email-review-pitfalls`. Read it when the draft's type is known. Source: the
> by-email-type reference of CSPulse's email-critic skill (MIT), rewritten in our own words; points marked own
> guidance are ours.

## 2.1 Meeting recap
1. **A recap carries six things**: what was discussed, what was decided, what the customer raised as a concern
   or priority, what we owe, what they owe, and what happens next with dates.
2. **It fails when the substance is sanded off.** The test: could the recap have been written about a different
   call with the same customer? If so, it lost the discussion.
3. **Every open item has an owner and, where one exists, a date.** "Next week" is not a date.
4. **The customer's concerns appear in their framing**, not translated into ours.
5. **Address each ask to the named person where it arises**, not in a block at the bottom everyone must read.
6. **Nothing is agreed in the email that was not agreed on the call**, and where the call reversed an earlier
   plan, the recap says so instead of leaving both versions live.

## 2.2 Follow-up and the third chase
1. **A follow-up answers four things**: why now, what is outstanding, why it matters to them, what you need.
2. **A passive check-in fails** because it puts the work of remembering on the customer and gives them nothing
   to react to.
3. **Is there a real reason to write** (new information, a moved date, something done on their behalf), or only
   a reason to nag?
4. **Make the ask small enough to do from a phone**, and show what minimal compliance looks like.
5. **A third chase on the same ask must change shape, not repeat**: escalate, remove the blocker for them, or ask
   what is in the way.

## 2.3 Bad news and escalation
1. **Order**: acknowledge the impact, own it, explain plainly, say where it stands, say what happens next and by
   when.
2. **It fails by going defensive** or by explaining internal process the customer did not ask about.
3. **Lead with their impact, not our explanation.**
4. **Ownership is real**, in the active voice; "the report was delayed" hides who owns it.
5. **Give a date for the resolution**; bad news without one reads as having no end.
6. **Apologise once.** The source's view: one clean apology lands, and several read as anxious and shift the
   customer into reassuring the sender.
7. **Outage and security notices have their own shape**; see `business/incident-communication` and
   `security-advisory-comms-pitfalls` (own guidance on routing).

## 2.4 The forwardable note
1. **Written because the customer asked for something to pass on** to legal, security or a colleague, who will
   forward it as it stands.
2. **It fails by carrying anything not meant for the final reader**, such as a scheduling ask or an unrelated
   thread; an email that needs editing does not get forwarded.
3. **Would it survive a reader who was not on the call** and does not know the sender?
4. **Business case before technical detail**: the forwarder needs to know why they pass it on, the specialist
   needs the substance.
5. **Anticipate the objection**, writing for the person looking for a reason to decline when the customer said
   most such requests are declined.
6. **Flag every claim about the product for verification.** The source calls this the type where a wrong claim
   does the most damage, because its readers are paid to find it and the cost lands weeks later.

## 2.5 Executive email
1. **The reader is senior, short of time and on a phone.** The point goes in the first two lines.
2. **Outcomes, not activity**: what changed, not what was done.
3. **One ask, and one only they can do.** If someone junior could act on it, it does not belong in their inbox.
4. **Make it scannable**; a wall of text gets its first line read and is archived.

## 2.6 Introduction request
1. **One paragraph, one ask, a line of thanks.** Senior people act on short emails and defer long ones.
2. **If it needs a second paragraph it is a briefing with an ask attached**, and should be split.
3. **The ask comes first after the greeting**, and it is clear what happens after the introduction so the
   introducer knows their work ends there.
4. **Keep the effort minimal**; the source notes that offering to draft the introduction often turns a maybe
   into a yes.
