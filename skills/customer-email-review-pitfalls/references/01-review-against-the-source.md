# customer-email-review-pitfalls §1 — Review against the source

> Part 1 of `skills/customer-email-review-pitfalls`. Read it when any customer-facing draft is reviewed. Source:
> the email-critic skill of CSPulse's customer-success skills (MIT), rewritten in our own words; points marked
> own guidance are ours.

## 1.1 Before judging
1. **Name what the email is for**: a recap, a nudge, an escalation, an ask, bad news, a note built to be
   forwarded. Each has its own shape and its own way of failing. If the purpose cannot be named from the draft,
   say so; the source's view is that an email whose purpose you cannot name is one the customer will not act on.
2. **Bad news the customer has not heard yet raises a prior question**: should this be an email at all. The
   source's strong version is a call first, with the email as the record afterwards.
3. **Get the source.** A draft cannot be judged against itself. Read, where they exist, the call transcript, the
   thread being replied into (what did the customer actually ask?), the account or contact notes, and a voice or
   style guide.
4. **Missing context does not block the review; it limits what the review may claim.** With a bare draft, say
   that the writing was reviewed and the facts were not, because most of the damage comes from a wrong fact and
   not from a clumsy sentence.

## 1.2 The checks that find real problems
1. **Accuracy** comes first. Does the draft claim something that did not happen? Does it upgrade "let's look at
   that" into "as agreed"? Does it say something was sent that cannot be found? Does it commit the sender to
   something not promised on the call?
2. **Completeness.** Does it drop what the customer will notice is missing: a concern they raised, a question
   they asked, an owner, a date? Compressing is good; a recap that quietly loses the customer's objection reads
   as not having listened.
3. **The ask.** Is there one, near the top, with a clear next step and a named person? Is the customer being
   asked to do work the sender could have done?
4. **The customer's read.** Read it once as the recipient: does it show they were heard, and does it sound like
   a partner or like a vendor chasing something? Would they reply today?
5. **Length against purpose.** The test is whether replying is easier than reading, not a word count. A short
   executive nudge that runs three paragraphs is too long; a security note of a page may be right.
6. **Voice** is judged against the workspace's own style guide, not generic taste. The source's house voice
   rules (a punctuation ban, banned phrases) are that repository's own and are not carried.

## 1.3 Say what you think, scaled to the draft
1. **Be specific and direct.** The useful sentences are of the kind "the ask is buried in the fourth
   paragraph", "this changes what was agreed on the call", "this is polite and will not get a reply".
2. **Do not manufacture criticism.** If the draft is good, the answer is "send it". A review that always finds
   three problems stops being trusted.
3. **Every criticism names the fix.** "Too long" is not feedback; "cut the second and third paragraphs, they
   restate the first" is.
4. **Match the weight of the answer to the draft**: send it, with a sentence and any small inline changes and no
   rewrite; nearly there, with a short verdict, the two or three changes and the revised email; needs work, with
   a verdict, what works, what does not, the biggest opportunity and the rewrite, the analysis shorter than the
   email; do not send, said first, with the reason and what the email should do before any rewriting, because
   the draft is often the wrong email, not a badly written one.
5. **Never give a numeric score.** A number invented by the same model that is about to rewrite is not a
   measurement and starts driving the edit, such as cutting words to justify a conciseness figure.

## 1.4 Rewriting
1. **Change what you named and nothing else.** If only two changes are needed, make two.
2. **Keep every fact, commitment, date, owner and nuance.** If shortening would lose one, keep it, cut
   elsewhere, and say what you kept.
3. **Never add a commitment, concern or next step.** A recap becomes the customer's record, so an invented next
   step becomes a real obligation.
4. **Keep the sender's voice.** A line that sounds like them stays, even if you would phrase it differently.
5. **Do not launder an unverified claim.** Pull it out and flag it.
6. **Check that the fix is executable.** The source's examples: telling someone to raise something at a meeting
   where most attendees have declined, to ask a person on leave, or to attach a document nobody has produced.
   Verify what the rewrite depends on (a meeting happening, a person available, a document existing) the way you
   verified the draft's claims.
7. **Close by naming what still needs the sender**: a fact you could not check, an address you could not find, a
   decision that is not yours.
