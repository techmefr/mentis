# customer-escalation-pitfalls §1 — First response

An escalation is the moment a problem moves above the level where you normally operate, on their side or
yours. The failure this section prevents is going quiet until the answer exists: silence is read as chaos or
concealment, and both are worse than an update with nothing new in it.

## 1.1 Name the kind first
1. **Three kinds need different handling**: a failure escalation (something broke or did not happen), a
   relationship escalation (nothing is broken today, accumulated frustration found a trigger) and a commercial
   escalation (the heat is real but the destination is a negotiation, often near a renewal or a budget cycle).
2. **Ask what specifically has to be true for this to be resolved.** A crisp technical answer means failure, a
   vague answer or a list means relationship, an answer about terms means commercial.
3. **A fix will not touch the cause of a relationship escalation**, and treating it as a failure escalation
   makes it worse; the account leaves later and the cause looks unrelated.
4. **Write the customer's answer down in their words**, as asked, not assumed.

## 1.2 Respond before you have the answer
1. **Send it inside the first hour**, before anything is known. The hour is the source's choice; set your own
   in the process.
2. **The shape has four parts**: acknowledge the specific impact in their terms ("this stopped your team
   invoicing for two days at month end", not "we understand your frustration"); say what you know and what
   you do not; name an owner who is a person, not a team; give the next update time, soon and specific.
3. **Leave three things out**: a theory of the cause, an apology for something not yet established, and a fix
   date you cannot hold.
4. **Then hold the cadence even with nothing new.** "No change, next update at four" is worth sending; the gap
   between updates is where the customer decides how seriously you take it.

## 1.3 Find out what they actually want
1. **Ask directly.** The wish is often not only the fix. The options the source lists: to be heard (the same
   thing was raised before and nothing changed), something to tell their own boss, a guarantee it will not
   repeat, compensation, an exit.
2. **Compensation is legitimate and is a commercial conversation**, not a service one; know which one you are
   in.
3. **Produce the forwardable written answer.** The contact is often not angry with you; they have been
   escalated to and need something defensible they can forward by a stated time. Writing it costs little and is
   often the most useful thing available.
4. **A fix delivered without the acknowledgement lands as dismissal** when the customer's point was that
   nothing had changed after earlier reports.

## 1.4 One owner, one channel, one version
1. **One named owner on your side sends everything customer-facing**; others feed that person. Three people
   responding in parallel with slightly different versions reads as evasion.
2. **One channel.** If it is running in email, a call and a shared channel at once, consolidate and say so.
3. **One written version of the facts, kept internally and updated as it changes**, so everyone who quotes
   it quotes the same thing.
4. **A running log** with time, what was learned, what was said to the customer and by whom, is the form this
   takes in the source's log template. Keeping it short enough to be kept is our addition.

## 1.5 Verify
Read the first response aloud against the three leave-outs in §1.2.3 and the four parts in §1.2.2, then check
that the next-update time in it was met. A first response that was not followed by the update it promised
failed the section whatever it said.
