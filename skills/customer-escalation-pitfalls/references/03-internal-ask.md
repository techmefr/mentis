# customer-escalation-pitfalls §3 — The internal ask

The other direction: you escalate inside your own company, usually on a customer's behalf, to people who did
not choose this account and are measured on something else. The failure this section prevents is "can someone
please look at this": no decision, no deadline and no owner, so it sinks in a queue.

## 3.1 Classify the ask and find who can say yes
1. **Four different things go to different people**: a decision (an exception, a discount, a priority call),
   work (a fix, an investigation), presence (an executive on a call) and cover (someone senior should know so
   the account is not a surprise).
2. **Cover is not an escalation**, and calling it one spends the word.
3. **Most failed internal escalations send a request for work to someone who can only give a decision, or the
   reverse.**
4. **Find who holds the specific authority**, not who is senior: the owner of the area by name, what they are
   measured on this quarter, whether they can decide alone, what they have already said.
5. **Use the existing intake path.** Going around it costs the relationship with the team you will need next
   month. If the path itself is broken, that is a separate escalation.

## 3.2 Shape the message and write the ask as a sentence
1. **Engineering** needs severity, scope, reproducibility, what breaks, the customer-visible symptom and what
   you have ruled out; not the emotional temperature.
2. **Leadership** needs revenue at stake, risk, precedent and the decision they are asked to make; not the
   technical detail. **Support** needs the history, so the customer does not explain it a fourth time.
3. **A message that reads as a forwarded customer complaint is handled as one.** Translate before sending.
4. **The form of the ask**: "I need NAME to ACTION by DATE, because CONSEQUENCE."
5. **Then give briefly**: what you have already tried (always the first question), the deadline and why it is
   real (name its source, such as a notice date or a board meeting), and what you can accept instead if the
   full ask is unavailable. A date invented for urgency is noticed quickly and remembered.

## 3.3 Do not inflate, and go one rung at a time
1. **Severity means what your company's definition says.** Marking something critical to get it looked at
   today only changes how your next one is read.
2. **Do not claim churn you would not defend**, and do not describe one angry user as the customer. Say "an
   annoyance on a healthy account" when it is one; that is what makes you believed on the one that matters.
3. **Start at the lowest rung that could solve it.**
4. **Tell the person before you go over them**, in one line ("I am raising this with X tomorrow, wanted you to
   hear it from me").
5. **Give each rung a real chance and a stated deadline**, said in advance so it is fair and not a threat.

## 3.4 Own it afterwards, and check the ask
1. **Escalation is not delegation.** Stay the customer's single point of contact.
2. **Keep the internal owner supplied with what they need and shielded from what they do not.**
3. **Update the customer on your own cadence**, not on the internal team's.
4. **Close the loop with everyone who helped, including when the answer was no.**
5. **Verify the ask before sending**: read it aloud; if it does not contain a name, an action and a date it is
   not finished, and it should say what you already tried and which kind of ask it is (§3.1).
