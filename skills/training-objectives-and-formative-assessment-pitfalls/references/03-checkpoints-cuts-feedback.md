# training-objectives-and-formative-assessment-pitfalls §3 — Checkpoints, cuts and feedback

All of this is from The Carpentries instructor training episodes unless marked "own guidance". The numbers
are their choices, shown as examples.

## 3.1 Formative assessment
1. **Check at the point an objective should be reached**, and design the check so everyone shows they got it
   and learners notice their own progress. Forms can be multiple-choice questions, an exercise, a quick
   question or sticky notes.
2. **Frequency**: ideally about every five minutes and at least every ten to fifteen, which the source ties
   to attention span and to breaking up long stretches of talk.
3. **"Do you understand?" is not an assessment.** The source says learners are reluctant to admit confusion
   and that people unfamiliar with a subject estimate their knowledge badly, so you get some inaccurate
   yeses and silence. Ask a targeted question or give a short exercise instead.
4. **Use a check before teaching too**: if everyone answers a question at the start correctly, skip the
   explanation they already know.
5. **Prepare to know the audience**: review the pre-session survey or questions, and imagine a real learner
   and what could be in their way. The source notes that you will never know everything about individuals.

## 3.2 Plan the cuts
Going slowly and pausing for checks usually means something will be cut. Plan it before the session.
1. **Keep breaks on time**, even if content is split awkwardly around them.
2. **Check dependencies**: a skipped step may create or rename something a later part uses.
3. **Leave time for the wrap-up**, even if that means cutting more.
4. **Do not speed up when behind.** Quickly covering without teaching gains little, especially when the
   material stays available afterwards.
5. **Tell your co-trainers what you plan to cut or left out**; do not assume anyone will notice.
6. **Tell the learners where to find what was skipped** and whether help is available; the source notes that
   a cut may make them anxious.

## 3.3 Feedback
1. **Anonymous feedback before long breaks** ("minute cards"): one positive and one constructive prompt, for
   example the most important thing learned and one thing that is confusing. Private and anonymous gives
   different and more useful feedback than a public board.
2. **Read it during the break, look for patterns and answer the common ones** at the start of the next part.
3. **Say what you did with it.** The source says learners give better feedback when they see it taken
   seriously.
4. **One up, one down at the end of a day**: people alternate a positive and a negative point without
   repeating what was said; requiring new points pushes past safe answers. Write it down without commenting
   while it is collected, then discuss how to act.
5. **Give time for the post-session survey inside the session**; the source found it is completed far more
   often there than after people leave.
6. **Review earlier feedback before the next session**, not only after the last one.

## 3.4 Practice
1. **Practice is varied attempts plus feedback and changed behaviour**, not repeating the same thing; the
   source warns that repetition mostly fixes bad habits.
2. **Prepare the learner-centred parts first**: the source observes that new instructors tend to over-prepare
   technical content and under-prepare how they will teach it.
3. **Own guidance**: the checks, cuts and feedback above also apply to a short internal session; keep them
   proportionate to its size.
