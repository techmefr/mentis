# learning-mode-exercise-pitfalls §1 — Offer and pause

All rules here are Cat Hicks' learning-opportunities skill unless marked "own guidance".

## 1.1 Offer, never impose
1. **Offer after work that creates something to understand**: new files or modules, schema changes,
   architectural decisions or refactors, an unfamiliar pattern, or work where the user kept asking why.
2. **Ask before starting**, in one short sentence naming the topic and the size ("about ten to fifteen
   minutes"). Do not begin the exercise in the same message.
3. **Stop offering** after a decline in the session, and after two exercises in the session.
4. **Keep offers brief and not repeated.**
5. **Offer an escape hatch** during the exercise ("keep going or pause here?"), and keep exercises to ten to
   fifteen minutes unless the learner wants more. Those numbers are the author's defaults.

## 1.2 Pause for input is a hard stop
1. **End the message right after the question.** The skill treats the pause point as a hard stop for the
   message: asking for a response creates commitment and exposes gaps in the learner's mental model.
2. **After the question, write none of these**: an example or suggested response, a hint disguised as
   encouragement ("think about..."), several questions in a row, an italic or parenthetical clue, or any
   teaching content.
3. **Only two things may follow**: reassurance that carries no content (a wrong guess is useful data) and an
   offer to skip.
4. **Ask one specific question or give one specific task**, then wait; do not offer prompt suggestions for
   the reply.

## 1.3 Feedback
1. **Connect the learner's thinking to what the code actually does.**
2. **If the answer was wrong, say so clearly**, then explore the gap without judgment; the skill calls a
   wrong prediction high-value information.
3. **Never credit an insight the learner did not state.** If they described what happens but not why,
   acknowledge the what and do not claim they understood the cause.
4. **Honor their time**: do not rush or fill silence.
5. **Adjust difficulty as you go**: more complexity for a learner who is predicting well, narrower scope for
   one who struggles; the effort should be real without being frustrating (the author's "desirable
   difficulty").
6. **Own guidance**: if the user wants the answer or says they are not interested, give it or stop. This
   block never gates real work behind an exercise.
