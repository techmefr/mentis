# recurring-update-writing-pitfalls §1 — Internal formats

Short formats a team repeats on a schedule. The sizes below are one example source's choices.

## 1.1 Progress, plans, problems
1. **Three sections**: progress (what the team got done), plans (what is top of mind for the next period)
   and problems (what slows the team: too few people, a blocker, a lost deal). Anthropic's internal-comms
   example calls this a "3P" update.
2. **Read in under a minute.** The example aims at 30 to 60 seconds, for readers who know a little but not
   much about what the team does, and keeps each section to one to three sentences.
3. **Cover the right period.** Progress and problems look back over the last period; plans look forward to
   the next one (same source).
4. **The bigger the team, the less granular the items.** A single team may report "shipped a feature", a
   whole company "hired twenty people" (same source).
5. **Data-driven and matter-of-fact**: include a metric where one exists and avoid prose-heavy writing.
6. **Know the team name and the dates covered** before writing; ask if they are not given.

## 1.2 Company newsletter
1. **Company-wide impact only.** The example says to prefer leadership announcements, major milestones,
   information that affects most employees and external recognition, and to leave out team-level detail
   (it belongs in the weekly update) and anything already communicated.
2. **Short bullets with links**: one or two sentences each, linking the document or announcement itself.
3. **Group by area** (for example product, go-to-market, finance, or external versus internal news) so each
   part of the company can find its own news.
4. **Write in the first person plural**, as "we did this", since the writer is part of the company.
5. **Use only what the writer can see.** The example source gathers items from chat, email, calendar and
   documents; own guidance: if those are not accessible, ask for the items and format them, do not guess
   what happened.

## 1.3 Short FAQ
1. **Choose questions that confuse many people**, usually about things that affect much of the company, not
   one team's.
2. **Question in one sentence, answer in one or two.**
3. **Base answers on official communications** where they exist and link them.
4. **Say clearly when the information is uncertain.**
5. **Flag a question that needs an answer from an executive** instead of answering it yourself.

## 1.4 Anything else
Anthropic's general guidance is to ask first about the audience, the purpose, the tone and any format
requirement; to be clear and concise, in active voice; to put the most important information first; and to
include links.
