# contract-review-pitfalls §3 — Triage and output

> Section 3 of `skills/contract-review-pitfalls`. Read it when findings must become a deliverable. Sources: the
> review-contract skill (commit `33eeda1`: deviation tiers, redline format, priority framework) and the
> contract-review skill (commit `bca0d2e`: acceptable-terms section, document status, guardrails), re-read
> 2026-10-16. The sources use a three-colour scale; we use plain words. Sentences marked "own guidance" are
> ours.

1. **Three levels, in words.** Each finding is acceptable, negotiate or escalate.
   - **Acceptable**: the clause matches or improves on the playbook position, or differs in a way the playbook
     allows. Noted, no action.
   - **Negotiate**: the clause is outside the standard position but inside what the playbook calls a range
     worth negotiating. It needs a redline and a fallback.
   - **Escalate**: the clause is outside the acceptable range, meets a trigger written in the playbook, or
     carries a material exposure. It goes to a person with authority.
2. **The thresholds come from the playbook.** Which side of a line a clause falls on is read from the
   organisation's written positions. Both sources print example figures (months of fees, notice days,
   uptime percentages, years of confidentiality); none of them gives a source for the figures and they differ
   between the two, so none is carried here. With no playbook, say so in the output and review against the
   generic baseline of part 1, with levels marked as provisional (own guidance).
3. **A negotiate item carries a record.** The exact current words; the proposed words; a reason of one or two
   lines that can be shown to the other side; a priority (must have, should have, or a concession
   candidate); a fallback if the first ask is refused. The first source's format has the same five parts.
   Proposed wording is a draft for counsel to adjust, not a final text.
4. **An escalate item names the decider and the exposure.** Who decides (counsel, a business owner with the
   authority to accept the risk) and what is at stake if the clause is accepted as written. The first source
   adds a recommended route; here the route is the named person.
5. **Order the negotiation.** The first source orders items as must-haves (cannot proceed without), strong
   preferences, and concessions that can be traded; its advice is to lead with the first, trade the third for
   the second, and not concede on the first without escalation. Keep that order, and say which items are
   linked.
6. **Always write the acceptable section.** List what was checked and found fine, per category. The second
   source requires it, and the reason is practical: the reader can see what the review covered, so silence
   is not read as "not looked at".
7. **Put completeness findings and the document state at the top.** Blank amounts, missing schedules, a draft or
   executed state, then the findings, then the acceptable section, then the next steps.
8. **Executed contracts are information only.** If the document is already signed, do not propose redlines as if
   they could be inserted; list the terms that matter and the dates that fall due (a notice window, a
   renewal date) and send questions to counsel (second source: the status of the document matters).
9. **No statement of enforceability.** Do not write that a clause is valid, void or enforceable. It varies by
   jurisdiction and belongs to counsel; the second source also asks to say when interpretation is unclear.
10. **End with the standing line.** The output closes by saying it is a working review for qualified counsel,
    not legal advice. Both sources do this; the second one also marks executed contracts as reviewed for
    information.
11. **Skip what the sources add that we do not carry.** Industry checklists for acquisitions, merchant payments
    and finder fees are narrow and US-centred; jurisdiction notes on non-competes and venues are claims about
    law that were not checked. If a review needs them, the playbook or counsel supplies them.
