# event-operations-pitfalls §1 — Selection and role

## 1.1 Goal and audience
1. **Write one primary goal**: meetings with target accounts, authority, community, or content. Symptom: an event
   justified by all four, measured by none. Fix: pick one, name the measure and the date it is read.
2. **Name who must be in the room and how many of them are there.** Ask the organiser for the attendee profile and
   check it against your target list; do not rely on the headline number.
3. **Ask whether being there in person is needed.** It earns its cost mostly for high-trust, high-value or
   regulated buying; elsewhere a digital format may do the same job for less. Decide with deal evidence
   (`business/b2b-go-to-market-pitfalls` §1.2).

## 1.2 Role
1. **Choose host, sponsor or exhibitor, speaker or attendee on purpose**, since each has a different cost and
   control. Mixed roles are normal; plan the follow-up once for all of them.
2. **Hosting**: the topic is the targeting. A subject your buyer would attend even if they never buy attracts the
   buyer; a talk about your product attracts customers. A recurring format builds an audience; a one-off
   disappears.
3. **Sponsoring**: ask for the previous year's attendee profile, what the package really includes and what
   sponsors reported. Do not pay for a logo with no access to the people.
4. **Speaking**: offer something the audience can use; a sales pitch loses the room and the organiser.
5. **Attending without a stand**: a target list and pre-booked meetings are often the best return for the cost.

## 1.3 Compare and decide
1. **Compare events by expected cost per useful conversation**, using your own past figures. If none exist, say
   the estimate is a guess and keep the first commitment small.
2. **A big event is not a better event.** Large shows dilute the audience; a small niche event can reach more of
   your targets. Judge by the attendee profile.
3. **Say no in writing, with the reason.** The no-list stops the same proposal returning each year.
4. **Review each event after, and keep the best few.** A handful usually produce most of the results; check that
   against your own data before concentrating.
