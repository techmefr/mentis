# community-moderation-ladder-pitfalls §3 — The ladder, decisions and appeals

What the response to a confirmed violation looks like, who may take each step, and how a person can
challenge it. A ladder that exists only in the moderator's head is arbitrary from the outside.

## 3.1 The steps
opensource.guide describes four responses. Use them as the rungs of a ladder, in this order of weight:
1. **A public warning**, in the channel where it happened where possible, explaining how the behaviour
   affected others; public communication shows the rest of the community that the rules are taken
   seriously. Be kind but firm.
2. **A private message to the person**, explaining the impact, suited to cases involving sensitive
   personal information. Copy the reporters only with their consent (§2.2).
3. **A suspension**, meaning a temporary ban on taking part in any aspect of the community. The article
   reaches for it when a resolution cannot be reached: the person turns hostile when confronted or does
   not change their behaviour.
4. **A permanent ban.** The article says banning is not to be taken lightly and represents a permanent
   and irreconcilable difference of perspectives; use it only when it is clear that no resolution can be
   reached.

Own guidance on shape, not taken from a licensed text: write the ladder down with, for each step, the
kind of event it answers, the consequence, any change of behaviour you ask for, and how the person can
repair the situation and return. Decide whether lower steps may be skipped for a severe event, and say
so in the published rules, so that skipping is not itself arbitrary.

## 3.2 Act at once, decide the heavy outcomes higher up
1. **Egregious violations and spam are removed immediately.** The Kubernetes moderation page says
   moderators are empowered to take immediate action without waiting for review, and that removing a bad
   actor or content is required: do not let it sit there.
2. **Removal of a person from the community is a decision above the moderator.** In that guide it comes
   from the code of conduct committee, which has final authority on escalated matters, not from the
   moderators. Own guidance: name your own body and write it next to the ladder.
3. **Block or ban for repeated violations, harassment or abuse, never for criticism or disagreement.**
   Apply it consistently, because arbitrary bans become their own story (social-media-skills moderation
   reference).
4. **Document why each decision was taken** (same reference). Own guidance: the log of §3.4 is where this
   lives.

## 3.3 Appeal and reinstatement are different requests
The GitHub appeal and reinstatement policy (CC0) separates the two, and the split is worth borrowing
whatever platform you run.
1. **Reinstatement:** the person wants access back, is willing to make the changes needed to address the
   violation and agrees not to repeat it. The platform may reinstate depending on circumstances and the
   severity of the first violation.
2. **Appeal:** the person disputes that a violation occurred and brings extra information to show a
   different decision was warranted.
3. **Grounds for reversing or changing a decision**, as that policy states them: the decision to act or
   not to act was mistaken; the content or conduct was not illegal and not incompatible with the terms;
   or the conduct warrants a lesser severity of action than the one taken.
4. **A human decides.** The policy says appeal decisions are not made by automated means, and that if the
   reviewer is the person who made the first decision and would lean towards denying, a different person
   reviews independently.

## 3.4 Windows, answers and records
1. **Set a window for appeals and write it down.** The GitHub policy accepts appeals up to six months
   after the decision and may refuse later ones. That is that platform's dated choice, read 2026-10-15;
   write your own window and do not copy the figure as a rule.
2. **Every legitimate request gets a final answer** (same policy). Own guidance: promise an answer date
   you can hold, as for report handling.
3. **Track appeals and reinstatements** so you can report on them; the GitHub policy points to its own
   public transparency page for this.
4. **Keep a log of reports and decisions**: what was reported, the verdict, the step taken and the reason.
   Own guidance, and the reason §2.3 and §3.2 refer back to it.
5. **Further legal rights to challenge a decision may exist depending on where the person lives.** That
   policy mentions an out-of-court dispute route in the European Union as an example. This block does
   not state what applies to you; ask legal.
