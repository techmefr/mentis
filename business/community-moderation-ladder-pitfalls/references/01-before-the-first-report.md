# community-moderation-ladder-pitfalls §1 — Before the first report

What a community owes its members before anyone has complained. Every later step in this block assumes
these exist; written for the first time during an incident they are improvised under pressure, and the
improvisation becomes the precedent.

## 1.1 Explain enforcement before a violation occurs
1. **Publish how enforcement works before the first violation.** The opensource.guide article on codes of
   conduct gives three reasons: it shows you intend to act when needed, members are reassured that
   complaints get reviewed, and members are reassured that the review is fair and transparent if they are
   ever the one investigated.
2. **State four things in the published rules**, each of which that article lists: where the rules take
   effect (only the issue tracker, or also events and chats), whom they apply to (members and
   maintainers, but what about sponsors), what happens on a violation, and how to report one.
3. **A rule that is not enforced, or cannot be, is worse than none.** The article quotes this from the Ada
   Initiative: it tells members the stated values are not respected. Own guidance: before publishing a
   rule, check there is a person with the access and the time to apply it on every surface it covers.
4. **Publish the rules where members already look**, and link them from the place new members land. Own
   guidance, not from a source.

## 1.2 Reports go through a private channel
1. **Give people a private way to report** (a dedicated address or form) and say who receives it: one
   person, a group, or a working group (opensource.guide).
2. **Name an alternative recipient.** Someone may need to report a person who normally receives the
   reports; without a second route, that person is unreportable (opensource.guide, which shows a project
   naming an outside contact for exactly this case).
3. **Reports are not posted as public replies.** A report written under the offending message exposes the
   reporter and invites an argument. Own guidance: say so in the published rules, with the private route
   next to it.

## 1.3 Decide who decides, in advance
1. **Write down who may act at once and who decides the heavy outcomes.** In the Kubernetes moderation
   guide, moderators act immediately on egregious violations without waiting for approval, while removal
   of a person from the community is decided by the code of conduct committee, not by the moderators
   (Kubernetes community repository, moderation page, "Roles and Responsibilities" and "Violations").
2. **Rules and ownership are part of the policy, not a side note.** The social-media-skills moderation
   reference lists prohibited content, response protocols (what is hidden or removed, and how fast),
   alignment with each platform's own rules, and who handles and escalates what. Every moderation decision
   should trace back to a clear public guideline.
3. **Consistency is the test.** The same source says arbitrary bans become their own story; a decision
   that cannot be traced to a published rule is the one that will be quoted against you (own guidance, not
   the source's wording). Where main
   `business/community-management` §1 and §3 already state the doctrine (published policy, consistency,
   never delete criticism), this block only adds the procedure after a report.

## 1.4 Law and platform duties
1. **Duties to remove illegal content and their deadlines vary by jurisdiction and platform, and were not
   verified here.** Do not copy a deadline or an article number from this block into a policy. Take them
   from the platform's own terms and from legal advice (`business/regulatory-watch`).
