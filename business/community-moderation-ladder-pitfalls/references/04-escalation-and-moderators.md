# community-moderation-ladder-pitfalls §4 — Large attacks, escalation and the people doing it

The case where one report becomes dozens of messages, and the case where the moderators are the thing
that wears out. Both are procedure problems: who does what in the first minutes, and who covers whom.

## 4.1 A large attack: two roles, not one
The Kubernetes moderation page splits the first response in two. Keep the split, not the names.
1. **The person on call only removes the offending content and asks the other on-call moderators for
   help.** That is their sole responsibility.
2. **A second person only documents the incident and finds help.** Their notes become the basis of a
   post-mortem.
3. **Escalate in a written order.** In that guide the second person escalates if needed: other primary
   moderators first for a sustained incident, then the owners of the affected subproject, then the code
   of conduct committee, then the steering committee, each "if appropriate". Write your own order.
4. **Keep a private contact list for the escalation path**, available to every moderator (same guide).
   Own guidance: test it. A list nobody has called is a guess.

## 4.2 After it
1. **Hold a post-mortem.** The source asks the collective moderators to report a one-off incident to the
   primary moderators within 24 hours. That figure is one project's choice; write your own deadline.
2. **Audit what the attack touched.** The same guide has the primary moderators audit the affected
   property afterwards (in its case a Slack emoji audit). Own guidance: list, per surface you moderate,
   what an attacker could have left behind.

## 4.3 What an escalation message carries
When a moderator escalates a conduct matter to a higher body, the Kubernetes page asks for:
1. **How serious and how urgent**: a statement of the seriousness and the urgency of response.
2. **The nature of the violation, with links**, plus a screenshot, because potentially ephemeral evidence
   can disappear.
3. **The relevant conversations already held** with the person in the moderator's capacity.
4. **Any relevant history.**

A message that omits one of these gets re-asked, which costs the time an urgent matter does not have.

## 4.4 Moderators are a limited resource
1. **Rotate primary moderators on a regular cycle.** The source says content moderation is personally
   tiring and has primary moderators review their positions as a team yearly: is the situation working,
   rotate accordingly, consider a less experienced person, consider time zones. The yearly cycle is its
   choice; write yours.
2. **Keep a lightweight standby role.** In that guide a pro tempore role covers events or absences and
   lets primary moderators step into it for a while as a healthy rotation.
3. **Cover time zones and absences as a group**, and tell your peers when you will be away (same guide).
4. **Step back when frustrated or in a heated debate, and watch colleagues for signs of burnout** (same
   guide, which links an external article on stages of burnout; that article was not read).
5. **Tell a person abusing the space from a person struggling to express themselves in the
   community's working language** (the guide says English; same guide), and apply compassion and empathy first in the second case.
