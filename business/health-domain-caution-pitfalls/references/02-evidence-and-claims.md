# health-domain-caution-pitfalls §2 — Evidence and claims

## 2.1 Reading a source before using it
1. **Go to the primary document**, not to the article about it. Symptom: a claim that the cited study never made.
   Check: find the sentence in the study that supports yours. Fix: restate what the study actually found, or drop
   the claim.
2. **Note who and what was studied**: which population, which dose or intervention, how long, compared with what.
   A result in one group is not a result in another. The FTC guidance asks that the studied product match the claimed one
   in dose, formulation, population and outcome, and that results not be stretched to another group or a wider benefit.
3. **Separate association from effect.** A link in observational data is not proof that one thing causes another;
   the wording must not suggest it does.
4. **A small, single or early study is a hint**, not a basis for a promise. Say how many took part and whether
   others have repeated it, after checking. The FTC guidance says replication strengthens evidence, that animal, lab and
   observational studies have limited value for a human health benefit, that post hoc subgroup analyses and selective
   outcome reporting do not substantiate a claim, and that a marketer may not rely on favourable studies while ignoring
   well-conducted contrary ones.
5. **Date every source and re-read it before relying**; guidance changes, and withdrawn papers exist.

6. **What counts as strong evidence is stated by the agency, not by us.** The FTC health-products guidance (re-read
   2026-10-20, US) says health-benefit claims generally need well-conducted, objectively evaluated human studies,
   usually randomised and controlled; that quality of research outweighs quantity; that no fixed number of trials is
   required but an independent replication adds weight; and that a study of a different strain, ingredient
   combination, extract or population does not carry over to our product. Symptom: a probiotic drink citing capsule
   studies of another strain. Check: dose, form, ingredients, outcome and group, one by one. Fix: claim only what the
   matching study showed. A US reference, not a rule for elsewhere.

## 2.2 Claims of treatment, prevention and cure
1. **Words like treats, cures, prevents, detoxes or boosts are claims** that a qualified owner must approve, and
   in many places a regulator controls them. Route them; do not decide them in copy.
2. **A wellness claim is still a claim.** Symptom: soft wording (supports, promotes) used to say the same thing.
   Fix: ask the same question of evidence. The FTC guidance treats an implied disease benefit, including through
   structure or function wording, images or product names, as a claim needing substantiation.
3. **Do not borrow a claim from a competitor, an influencer or a customer review.** A testimonial about health is a
   claim by the company once it is published (FTC guidance: testimonials count as claims, and material connections
   between endorser and company must be disclosed). Using third-party articles to promote a product can make the
   company responsible for what they imply.
4. **Do not compare against a treatment** without evidence of comparison and review.

## 2.3 Numbers and risk
1. **Say absolute numbers beside relative ones** when a risk change is discussed: a large relative change on a rare
   event is still rare. Re-read the source figures before quoting.
2. **Name the time frame and the group** for any statistic.
3. **Do not round a result towards the claim.**
4. **Do not use a figure you cannot trace**; mark it unchecked and keep it out of public copy.
5. **Show benefits and risks together and say what is known and not known.** The CDC Clear Communication Index scores
   whether a material states one main message and a call to action, uses numbers readers can follow, explains what the
   numbers mean, presents risks and benefits together, and separates what is known from what is unknown (CDC Clear
   Communication Index page; a US public-health tool, used here for its checklist only).

## 2.4 Anecdotes and testimonials
1. **A story is not evidence.** The FTC guidance says individual experiences, consumer surveys and practitioner
   observations do not substantiate efficacy. If published, label it as one person's experience, get written consent, and
   remove anything that identifies someone without it (`business/data-protection`).
2. **Never invent or composite a patient story** and present it as real.
3. **Check what a customer says about their condition for claims** before it is quoted.
