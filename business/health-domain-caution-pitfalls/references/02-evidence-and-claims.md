# health-domain-caution-pitfalls §2 — Evidence and claims

## 2.1 Reading a source before using it
1. **Go to the primary document**, not to the article about it. Symptom: a claim that the cited study never made.
   Check: find the sentence in the study that supports yours. Fix: restate what the study actually found, or drop
   the claim.
2. **Note who and what was studied**: which population, which dose or intervention, how long, compared with what.
   A result in one group is not a result in another.
3. **Separate association from effect.** A link in observational data is not proof that one thing causes another;
   the wording must not suggest it does.
4. **A small, single or early study is a hint**, not a basis for a promise. Say how many took part and whether
   others have repeated it, after checking.
5. **Date every source and re-read it before relying**; guidance changes, and withdrawn papers exist.

## 2.2 Claims of treatment, prevention and cure
1. **Words like treats, cures, prevents, detoxes or boosts are claims** that a qualified owner must approve, and
   in many places a regulator controls them. Route them; do not decide them in copy.
2. **A wellness claim is still a claim.** Symptom: soft wording (supports, promotes) used to say the same thing.
   Fix: ask the same question of evidence.
3. **Do not borrow a claim from a competitor, an influencer or a customer review.** A testimonial about health is a
   claim by the company once it is published.
4. **Do not compare against a treatment** without evidence of comparison and review.

## 2.3 Numbers and risk
1. **Say absolute numbers beside relative ones** when a risk change is discussed: a large relative change on a rare
   event is still rare. Re-read the source figures before quoting.
2. **Name the time frame and the group** for any statistic.
3. **Do not round a result towards the claim.**
4. **Do not use a figure you cannot trace**; mark it unchecked and keep it out of public copy.

## 2.4 Anecdotes and testimonials
1. **A story is not evidence.** If published, label it as one person's experience, get written consent, and
   remove anything that identifies someone without it (`business/data-protection`).
2. **Never invent or composite a patient story** and present it as real.
3. **Check what a customer says about their condition for claims** before it is quoted.
