# project-management-artefact-pitfalls §3 — Status, change and closure

> Part 3 of `business/project-management-artefact-pitfalls`. Read it when a report, a change request or a
> close-out is written.

A status report is read by people who decide something because of it. It fails when it is optimistic by habit,
when it hides the one fact that needs a decision, or when a change happens without anyone choosing it.

## 3.1 Report on evidence
1. **Back every colour with a fact.** Symptom: green for twelve weeks then red the week of the deadline. Fix:
   each status states the evidence (milestone met, test passed, decision taken) and what would turn it amber. A
   colour with no stated criterion is an opinion.
2. **Show the trend, not only the state.** Own guidance: the same amber for three weeks is a different message
   from amber after green. Fix: one line on the direction since the last report.
3. **Report done as done.** Symptom: "90% complete" for a month. Check: is there an observable finish? Fix: count
   finished items and list what is left, instead of a percentage nobody can verify.
4. **Lead with what changed and what is needed.** Put the one-line state first, then decisions, then risks, then
   detail. A reader who stops after three lines should still know whether to act.
5. **End with decisions asked, each with an owner and a date.** Symptom: the update is read and nothing happens.
   Fix: "Decision needed from the sponsor by the 15th: A or B; if none, B by default."
6. **One report, one source.** Own guidance: if the plan, the log and the report each say something different,
   the reader trusts none. Fix: the report is built from the log and the plan, not retyped from memory.

## 3.2 Bad news
1. **Say it when you know, and with a proposal.** Symptom: a slip kept back "until we have a plan". Fix: report
   the slip now, say what is known and unknown, and attach the options.
2. **Separate the fact from the cause and the blame.** The report says what happened and what it affects; the
   cause analysis follows once facts are agreed.
3. **Escalate on a rule agreed in advance.** Write the thresholds (a milestone missed by more than a stated
   amount, a risk crossing a stated level) at the start, so an escalation is a rule applied, not a political
   act.

## 3.3 Change control
1. **A date or scope change is a decision, written down.** Symptom: the end date in the plan differs from the one
   sponsors remember. Fix: record what changed, why, what it costs in time, money and other scope, who decided,
   and when.
2. **Trade explicitly.** Adding something means removing something, moving the date or adding capacity. Never
   absorb it silently into "the plan".
3. **Keep the baseline.** Keep the first agreed plan alongside the current one, so the size of the drift is visible
   and nobody has to argue about it. The GAO guide treats the baseline as the target under change control, with a
   basis document that records assumptions and justifies constraints, lags and long durations, and measures
   performance against it (practice 10).
4. **Rebaseline rarely, and say what it erases.** The GAO guide says a rebaseline normally clears the historical
   variances so new ones show up, that it should be rare, and that frequent rebaselining suggests unclear scope or
   weak estimating discipline. Symptom: a plan that is rebaselined at every steering committee and never late.
   Fix: keep the old baseline in the record and require the decision of 3.3.1 for each rebaseline (practice 10).
5. **Small changes need a rule too.** Decide in advance which changes the delivery lead may accept alone and which
   go up; a slow process for tiny changes gets bypassed.

## 3.4 Closing
1. **Close against the agreed outcome, not the plan.** Check what was promised, what was delivered, what was left
   out and who accepted the gap.
2. **Hand over what keeps running.** Operations, support, documentation, licences and the open items go to a named
   owner with a date; a project that ends and leaves a service with no owner has only moved its risk.
3. **Write what to do differently, as actions with owners.** Symptom: a lessons list that lists feelings. Fix:
   each lesson becomes a change to a template, a checklist or a practice, with an owner; one that cannot is
   dropped. A review without blame follows `skills/blameless-incident-review-pitfalls`.
4. **Close the log.** Open risks and issues are transferred or closed with a reason; none are left to vanish.
