# content-brief-writing-pitfalls §1 — Intent, overlap and information gain

## 1.1 Reader and question first
1. **Write the reader and the question in one sentence each**, in the words the reader uses. Symptom: a brief
   headed by a keyword and a word count. Check: could two different writers produce the same piece from this?
   Fix: add the situation the reader is in and what they will do after reading.
2. **Classify what the reader expects**: a quick answer, a how-to, a comparison, a definition, a tool or a template.
   Open the results the query already returns and note the format that dominates; a format mismatch loses even a
   good piece.
3. **Use customer language from real sources** (support questions, sales objections, interview notes), not a
   keyword tool alone. Mark the source of each.
4. **Searchable or shareable, say which.** A piece that answers existing demand and a piece that creates attention
   are briefed differently; a brief that wants both usually delivers neither.

## 1.2 Overlap and cannibalisation
1. **Keep a map of query to page.** Symptom: two pages swap places for one query, or neither ranks. Check: search
   the site for the topic and its close phrasings before commissioning. Fix: one owner page per query.
2. **When a new piece overlaps, choose**: extend the existing page, merge the two and redirect, or give the new
   piece a clearly different question. Write the choice in the brief.
3. **Cluster by question, not by word variants.** Singular and plural, or two synonyms, are the same question; do
   not give each a page.
4. **Plan the internal links in the brief**: the page it supports, the pages that should point to it, and the label
   to use.
5. **Prefer a redirect to a hint when retiring a duplicate.** The search guidance read ranks consolidation methods: a
   permanent redirect is the strongest, a canonical annotation is a signal the engine may overrule, a sitemap entry is
   weaker; choose one canonical method and keep internal links pointing at the chosen address. Duplicate or very similar
   pages are consolidated under one preferred address, which also combines the signals (links) and simplifies
   tracking. The pages read do not define cannibalisation or promise any ranking effect, so the term here is a working
   one for two of your pages answering one question (Google Search Central, duplicate URLs).

## 1.3 Information gain
1. **Read the current top results for the query** and write one sentence on what this piece will add: original data
   from your own work, a real example, a working template, a clearer answer, a defensible opinion. Symptom: a brief
   that summarises competitors. If the sentence cannot be written, drop the topic. A search provider's guidance on helpful content asks the same
   questions: does the page offer original information or analysis, does it add substantial value over other results
   and over the sources it draws on, rather than rewording them (Google Search Central, helpful content).
2. **The addition must come from something you can show.** An internal measurement or a real case qualifies;
   an adjective does not (`business/content-creation` §2).
3. **Check that the addition is wanted.** A new angle nobody asks about is a hobby; tie it to the reader's question.
4. **Do not promise a ranking or a number of visits**; some formats attract links and some do not, and any study of
   which is directional only. Re-read any figure at its source and date it before quoting.
