# § 1 - Content that is trusted, by readers and by search engines

> Section 1 of `business/content-creation`. Read it when a piece will live on a page meant to be found
> through search (an article, a guide, a documentation page, a post on the company site), or when a
> page's editorial quality is being audited. The technical side of being indexed is `skills/seo`; the
> machine-writing filter is `business/ai-prose-tells`.

Search engines say the same thing about content that careful editors always said: write for the people
who will read it, and be able to show why they should believe it. The points below restate the public
guidance of a search provider (Google Search Central: its pages on helpful, reliable, people-first content,
on spam policies and on generative AI content, read 2026-10-02). Ranking systems change; the editorial
questions do not, so this section states questions and habits, never a ranking factor or a number.

## People first

1. **Start from an audience that would want this if search did not exist.** Name who it serves and what
   they leave knowing or able to do. A piece made mainly to attract search visits, or to cover a trending
   topic the team would not otherwise write about, is the pattern to avoid.
2. **Self-assess with questions, not tools.** Does it add original information, a measurement, analysis or
   first-hand experience? Does it go beyond what a reader would already guess? If it draws on other
   sources, does it add value beyond summarising them? Does the title describe the page honestly, without
   exaggeration? Would you bookmark it, send it to a colleague, expect to see it cited? Is it carefully
   produced, free of obvious errors? Ask someone outside the team to answer the same questions.
3. **Say no to the topic where we have no experience.** Entering a subject only because it draws traffic
   produces thin pages. The credibility list in `SKILL.md` §1 decides what we write about.
4. **Length follows the question.** There is no preferred word count; writing to a length target is a
   warning sign. A short page that answers fully beats a long one that circles.
5. **A promise the page cannot keep is a defect.** A title that implies a release date that has not been
   confirmed, or a question with no answer, produces a reader who leaves and searches again.

## Showing why to trust it

6. **Make experience and expertise visible.** A named author with a short, true description of what they
   did or know; a link to a page about who they are; the first-hand detail (what was run, measured,
   built). Trust is the most important of the signals the guidance describes, and the others (experience,
   expertise, authoritativeness) feed it.
7. **Cite sources a reader can open.** For each factual claim that is not first-hand, link to the primary
   source, named by what it is, not "source" or "here" (`business/ux-writing` §6.1). Quote sparingly and
   in quotation marks; a long extract is a licensing question (`business/licence-compliance`).
8. **Disclose how it was made when it helps the reader.** Where automation or generative tools were used,
   say how, in a way that makes sense for the audience. This is context for the reader, not a confession.
9. **Dates are true.** Show the publication date and, if there is one, the date of a substantial update.
   Never change a date to look fresh when the content did not substantially change, and do not add or
   remove pages in bulk to seem fresh. Freshness is the result of keeping content right.
10. **A page about us and a way to reach us exist, and are real.** Who is behind the site, how to
    contact them, how corrections are made. A short editorial note (how we choose topics, how we check
    claims, how we correct mistakes) is cheap and answers questions a sceptical reader has.
11. **A disclaimer informs; it does not replace accuracy.** Where advice could affect health, money, safety
    or someone's legal position, the piece is written or reviewed by a person who is competent in the
    subject, and says so. Such topics are held to a higher standard; if we have no such person, we do not
    publish on the subject (`business/legal-documents`, `business/fintech-compliance` for our own
    regulated territory).

## Generated and scaled content

12. **Generated text is allowed; unreviewed generated text is not.** Generative tools can structure and
    research; they predict likely words and can state falsehoods fluently. Every factual statement is
    checked by a person before publication. The review includes the parts that appear in search results
    and are easy to forget: the title element, the description, structured data and image alternative text.
13. **Many pages without value to a reader is the failure.** Generating, scraping, stitching or
    translating content in bulk to cover many queries, without adding something for the reader, is treated
    as a spam practice (scaled content abuse), however it is produced. A hundred pages need a hundred
    reasons to exist.
14. **Repetition for a search engine is stuffing.** Repeating a phrase until it sounds unnatural, listing
    cities or regions a page hopes to rank for, or pasting lists of numbers is a spam practice and reads as
    one. Use the words the reader uses, once where they fit, and use synonyms because the prose needs
    them, not as a technique.
15. **Paid and user-supplied links are labelled.** An advertisement or paid placement carries a
    sponsored relationship on the link, a link inside a comment or forum post carries a user-generated
    one, and a link we do not want to vouch for carries a nofollow value; the attribute names and their
    current use are in the provider's guidance on qualifying outbound links. Never buy or sell links to
    influence ranking, or exchange links as a scheme.
16. **Internal and external links serve the reader's next step.** An internal link points at the next
    thing the reader probably wants, with text that says what it is; an external link goes to the source
    that supports the sentence it sits in.

## Check

17. **Before publishing, answer in writing:** who is this for, what original thing does it contain, who is
    named as the author and why they are credible, which sources back each claim, how it was produced
    and who checked it, and when it was last materially updated. An answer missing from the page is
    either added or the piece waits.
