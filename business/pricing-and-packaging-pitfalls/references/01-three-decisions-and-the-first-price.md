# pricing-and-packaging-pitfalls §1 — Three decisions and the first price

A pricing meeting goes wrong when it argues about a number before anyone has said what is being sold in which
box and what the price scales with. This section separates the decisions and says what competitor prices and
costs are good for.

## 1.1 Three decisions, in this order
1. **Packaging**: what is included in which plan (features, limits, support, access).
2. **Metric**: the unit the price scales with (a seat, a unit of usage, a feature, a flat fee).
3. **Number**: how much, per unit and per plan.
4. **Write them as three separate lines with an owner each.** A number chosen first forces a metric that
   makes it work, and the metric then punishes the wrong customers.
5. **Revisit in the same order.** A complaint about the number is often a complaint about the metric; check
   §2 before moving the number.

## 1.2 What competitor prices and costs are
1. **A competitor's price is a data point about their strategy and cost base.** Matching it copies their
   choice, not their economics. Use it to see the alternatives a buyer compares, not as the target.
2. **Cost to serve is a floor.** It tells you where a plan loses money, never what to charge.
3. **Value-based pricing needs a measurable value driver** (time saved, cost removed, revenue gained, in the
   customer's own numbers). Without one it collapses into "whatever they will pay", which is a guess with a
   better name. If you cannot measure the driver, say the price is not value-based.
4. **The price sits between the next best alternative the buyer has and the value they perceive.** Write the
   alternative and the perceived value in words before writing the number.

## 1.3 The first price is a bet
1. **Pick a price the market can contradict cheaply.** The aim of a first price is to learn.
2. **Write the hypothesis and a review date** next to it: who should buy at this price, and what you will look
   at to know you were wrong.
3. **Avoid the ultra-low price.** It produces signups that would never have paid a real price, and it is hard
   to raise later because the first customers set expectations. The order-of-magnitude slogans in circulation
   ("start at ten, a hundred or a thousand") and "just charge fifty and see" are anecdote: keep only the logic
   that a first price is an experiment.
4. **Do not tune a first price with a survey alone.** A survey gives a range (§5); the market gives the
   answer.

**Never** open with a number. Write the packaging and the metric first, and bring the number last with its
hypothesis.
