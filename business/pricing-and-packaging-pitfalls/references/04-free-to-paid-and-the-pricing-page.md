# pricing-and-packaging-pitfalls §4 — Free to paid, and the pricing page

How a customer starts paying is a hypothesis with a cost model, not a default. The page is where the
structure becomes visible, so it gets a checklist.

## 4.1 Three shapes and what each costs
1. **Free plan.** You pay to serve people who may never pay. Raising prices later is harder because the free
   baseline anchors expectations. It needs a real upgrade trigger (§3.1) and a low marginal cost to serve.
   It fits when free users bring something back (referrals, content, data, network effect).
2. **Trial.** It fits a product that needs time to show value or setup effort before it does, a buying
   committee, or a sales-assisted sale. It needs a stated moment when value should be visible.
3. **Reverse trial.** Full access first, then a downgrade to a limited plan. The customer sees the full value
   and lives with the loss; it needs the limited plan to be a plan you can afford to serve.
4. **Decide before choosing:** who pays for non-converters (the budget line), and what the time to value is
   (days to a first useful result, in your own data). Write both.
5. **Trial length and card-upfront effects are measured on your product, not borrowed.** The sources quoted
   conversion figures for them without a derivation; none is kept here. If you need to decide, run the test
   (`skills/experiment-design-pitfalls`) and read retention, not only trial-to-paid.

## 4.2 The pricing page checklist
Each plan states:
- who it is for;
- what is included, and what is excluded;
- the limits, in text and not only in an image or a column of ticks;
- the price and the billing period;
- what happens on overage and on cancellation;
- taxes and mandatory fees (the display rule and the tax side are `skills/vat-handling-in-software-pitfalls`
  and the lawyer's call, not this block's).

Across the page:
- a published anchor for a contact-sales plan, or a stated reason for not publishing one;
- every claim next to the price (a guarantee, a saving, a customer count) has a source
  (`business/marketing-claim-substantiation-pitfalls`);
- a plain statement of how a customer changes or leaves a plan (the mechanics of changes belong to
  `business/price-change-and-billing-pitfalls`).

The sources also raise a point about readability of prices by automated agents (prices as text, structured
offer data). It is plausible, but its rubric is not summarised here because it rests on one source and a
heuristic test, not read in full against independent references.

**Never** write the page from the checklist alone. Read it as a buyer in the segment the plan is for, and ask
what they still cannot tell.
