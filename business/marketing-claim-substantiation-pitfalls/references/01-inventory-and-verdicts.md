# marketing-claim-substantiation-pitfalls §1 — Inventory and verdicts

Main `business/product-marketing` §2 states the principle: every factual claim gets a source before it
ships. This section is the procedure that turns the principle into a table somebody can sign.

## 1.1 Extract every claim first
1. **A claim is any assertion a buyer could dispute**: a number, a superiority statement, an outcome
   promise, a category claim (pmalliance claim-check skill).
2. **Collect claims from every surface**, not only the page being changed: homepage, product pages,
   pricing page, sales deck, one-pagers, battlecards, ad and email copy (same skill).
3. **Collect the proof at the same time**: reviews and testimonials, case studies, call transcripts where
   customers describe results, product data and benchmarks, analyst mentions (same skill). The skill's own
   caveat: checking claims in batches by theme can miss support that belongs to another theme, so keep
   the whole proof set in view.
4. **Quote each claim as written and name the surface it came from**, so the table can be checked by
   someone who has not read the copy (same skill's output format).

## 1.2 Give each claim a verdict
1. **Three verdicts**: supported (naming the specific proof), thin (some support, not enough),
   unsupported (nothing found) (pmalliance).
2. **"Supported" requires a named piece of proof**: the review, case study, transcript line or dataset.
   "It is probably true" is not support; with no proof found the verdict is unsupported, full stop.
3. **Do not soften a verdict to reassure** the people who wrote the claim (same skill).
4. **Per thin or unsupported claim, three possible fixes**: get evidence, reword, or remove. A suggested
   rewording must be something the evidence that exists can carry (same skill).
5. **Reconcile with the main block.** Main `business/product-marketing` §2 says an unsourced claim is
   removed, not softened. Own guidance, to keep both true: rewording is allowed only when the new
   sentence is a smaller claim that the proof really supports; it is then a different claim and gets its
   own row. Softening the adjectives of the same unsupported claim is not a fix.

## 1.3 Regulated and comparative claims
1. **Flag claims that read as regulated or comparative and send them to legal or compliance review**
   instead of judging them yourself (pmalliance).
2. **Claim law varies by country and sector and was not verified here.** This block names no statute or
   authority. Main `business/product-marketing` §2.4 already routes compliance and security words to the
   person who owns them.

## 1.4 The report
1. **A summary**: number of claims checked, split by verdict, riskiest claims first.
2. **A claim table**: claim as written, source surface, verdict, supporting proof with its source or
   "none found", suggested fix (pmalliance).
3. **A fix list** for every thin or unsupported claim, and **review flags** for claims needing legal or
   compliance sign-off (same skill).
4. **A flagged item is never carried forward as "conditionally fine".** ContentForge's fact-checker gate
   has no conditional pass with open flags: a flagged source or statistic is replaced or removed, or the
   work goes back. Own guidance: the same holds for a claim row marked unsupported before publishing.

## 1.5 What regulators' pages say about substantiation (read 2026-10-20, facts only, US and UK examples)
1. **Prior substantiation is the principle.** The US trade commission's policy statement says an advertiser needs
   a reasonable basis for an objective claim before it runs, that an express claim such as "tests prove" needs
   at least that level of support, that an implied claim needs the support the ad implies, and that evidence
   created after the claim does not excuse its absence. This confirms 1.2.2 (no proof, no "supported") and the
   removal of unsupported claims. Whether and how it binds us depends on the country: legal decides (1.3).
2. **Net impression counts.** The commission's small-business FAQ says an ad is read as a reasonable consumer
   would read it, words and pictures together, and that omissions can mislead; qualifying information must be
   clear, near the claim and must not contradict it. Check each claim's surrounding image and footnote, not only
   the sentence.
3. **A testimonial is not proof of a claim.** The commission's endorsement pages say endorsements cannot be used
   to make claims the advertiser could not make itself, an endorser's unusually good results need a statement of
   what is typical unless typicality is proven, and an advertiser answers for what its agents and affiliates say.
   Check: who wrote it, for what, and what the typical result is.
4. **Comparative claims need evidence about both sides.** The UK advertising regulator's page (read 2026-10-20)
   says marketers must hold documentary evidence before publishing objective claims, judged by how consumers
   would understand them and not by intent; testimonials and self-reported data alone are not enough for
   objective claims; and a comparison needs evidence covering our product and every competitor named, on
   material, relevant, verifiable and representative features. Its worked example is a "best on the market" claim
   that failed because the evidence covered a few competitors and few features. This is the sort of claim
   1.3 sends to legal.
