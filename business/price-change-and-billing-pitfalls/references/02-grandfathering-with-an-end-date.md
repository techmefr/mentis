# price-change-and-billing-pitfalls §2 — Grandfathering is a decision with an end date

Keeping existing customers on an old price feels kind, and it is often the right call for a while. The failure is
to leave it unwritten: nobody knows who is covered, for how long, or what ends it, and the gap between the old
and the new price is carried forever without anyone having chosen to.

## 2.1 The record
For every group kept on an old price, write five lines:
1. **Who**: the rule that puts an account in the group (signed before a date, on a named plan), and the list that
   follows from it.
2. **At what price**: the exact old terms, including what is bundled.
3. **Until when**: an end date, or a rule that ends it (the plan changes, the contract renews, the seat count
   passes a point). "Until further notice" is not an end.
4. **What triggers the move**: the event after which the new price applies, and who tells the customer.
5. **What the customer may do instead**: stay on the old plan until the date, move early, or leave.

## 2.2 The gap is closed on a schedule
A customer below the new price is a gap, not a loss. Do not total the gap into a headline of money "lost":
that figure assumes every grandfathered account would have paid the new price, which the rollout read (§1)
has not shown. Close the gap on the dates in the record and review the record each cycle.

## 2.3 Read the contracts
Some contracts freeze a price for a term, require notice before a change, or fix how an increase is calculated.
Which accounts have such terms, and what they say, is a reading of the signed contract by the person who owns
legal (`business/legal-documents` for drafting questions), not a guess from the plan name. List the accounts
whose terms were not checked as unknown; do not move them.

## 2.4 New promises
Every "we will never raise your price" said by a seller, in a page or in an email is a grandfathering rule with
no end date. Search for them before a change and add them to the record.
